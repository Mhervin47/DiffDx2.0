The DiffDx field guide

Not the pitch, not the research note — the whole thing. How a conversation becomes a differential, how a differential becomes a specialist recommendation instead of a diagnosis, and everything the product actually does once a patient has a doctor of their own.

Reference ~20 min read Architecture + product, end to end

DiffDx is a full patient-doctor telehealth product built around an AI-conducted diagnostic interview. Three model roles run every session — an actor that asks questions, a critic that grades them live, and a router that decides who the patient should see. Everything downstream of that — booking, messaging, records, oversight — is built to the same rule: the AI proposes, a licensed human confirms, and nothing is hidden from the people responsible for it.

01 — what this isOne conversation, three parties

A patient opens a chat, describes what's wrong, and answers questions for a few minutes. Underneath, that's a live diagnostic session: an adaptive interview producing a ranked differential that updates after every answer. On top of it sits everything a real clinic needs — booking a real doctor, a chart that survives past the conversation, messaging, test results, and a console for the people responsible for the AI's behavior in the first place.

The three parties never share one screen. A patient sees guidance. A doctor sees the full transcript with the AI's own self-grading attached. An admin sees neither patient nor doctor data by default — they see the system: audit trail, cost, safety recall, and a queue of anything either side has flagged.

02 — architectureThree loops, one session

Every session runs through three independent model roles. They don't share a prompt, and the second one's entire job is to find fault with the first.

Loop 1 · Actor
Doctor LLM
Asks one question at a time, updates a live differential
Loop 2 · Critic
Turn scorer
Grades every turn, in a background thread, live
Loop 3 · Router
Urgency + specialty
Runs once, after the interview ends

Why a separate critic, not one model grading itself

A model scoring its own output tends to agree with itself. The critic is a distinct call, blind to the ground truth, judging the question that was asked — not whether the session eventually landed on the right answer. It runs on every real conversation in production, not as an offline eval script.

03 — the engineLoop 1: the doctor turn

Each turn, the actor receives the accumulated patient state and produces exactly one question — never a bundle of several. It pulls similar past cases through vector retrieval, compresses older turns once a conversation runs long so the prompt doesn't grow without bound, and checks a small, explicit set of red-flag patterns on every single patient answer before doing anything else.

  1. Safety check firstEvery answer is screened for a red flag — chest pain radiating to the arm, can't breathe — before the actor does anything else with it.
  2. Retrieve exemplarsA small set of similar past cases is pulled by relevance and diversity, so the actor isn't just handed near-duplicates of one case.
  3. Compress if neededOnce a conversation runs long, older turns are summarized so context stays bounded without losing the clinically relevant thread.
  4. Ask one questionThe model outputs a single question, an updated differential, and a rationale that has to reference something actually said in the conversation.
  5. Decide whether to stopConfidence, a turn limit, or a safety trigger — whichever comes first — ends the interview and hands off to the router.

A hard stop, not a soft one

A red-flag hit ends the interview immediately and routes straight to an emergency message. It bypasses the differential, the critic, and the router entirely — safety doesn't wait for the rest of the pipeline to agree.

04 — the judgeLoop 2: the critic

A second, independent model grades every turn on four axes — question quality, differential quality, reasoning, and calibration — in a background thread, so a slow grade never holds up the patient's next question. This isn't an eval-time artifact; it's an automated QA layer sitting over every real clinical decision the actor makes, live.

Question quality
Was this the highest-yield question to ask at this specific moment in the conversation, given what's already known?
Differential quality
Does the updated probability distribution actually reflect the evidence accumulated so far — not just move in a plausible direction?
Reasoning
Does the stated rationale reference specific dialogue evidence and name the hypothesis it's testing, rather than a generic justification?
Calibration
Does stated confidence track how much the differential has actually narrowed — an overconfident early turn is a real failure mode, not a stylistic one.

The same critic machinery doubles as the ground-truth evaluator for the project's offline DDXPlus evaluation harness — one scoring engine, two jobs: judging real sessions live, and judging simulated ones offline for regression testing.

05 — the handoffLoop 3: routing, safety first

Once — not per turn — the router takes the finished differential and classifies both an urgency level and a specialty, using a disease-to-specialty map with a fallback path for differentials that don't clearly point to one specialty.

06 — the productA session, start to finish

  1. IntakeSigned in or as a guest — demographics and a chief complaint. No account required to start; only to keep the result.
  2. The interviewOne question at a time, with the live differential visible in a collapsible sidebar as it narrows.
  3. TerminationConfidence threshold, turn limit, or a safety trigger — whichever comes first, labeled plainly: "completed full assessment," not a status code.
  4. The reportLeads with a specialist recommendation and urgency — never a named diagnosis. The full differential is one collapsed section away.
  5. Suggested testsGenerated once, then re-checked automatically on every later visit — no button, a quiet "New" badge if anything additional turns up.
  6. BookingA database-level constraint makes double-booking the same doctor at the same slot structurally impossible, not just application-checked.

07 — a deliberate choiceThe report never says a disease name

This was a direct response to mentor feedback: a system that explicitly isn't a diagnosis shouldn't hand a patient a disease name anyway. Naming one thing — correctly or not — can trigger real panic before a licensed doctor ever weighs in.

What the patient sees vs. what exists

The report headline is "See an Internal Medicine specialist," with an urgency badge and a plain- language explanation of why. The AI's actual differential, confidence scores, and turn-by-turn critique still exist in full — behind one collapsed "Technical Details" section meant for clinicians and reviewers, not the patient's first read.

08 — the long tailLife after booking

A diagnostic session ending is the easy part. What makes this a real clinical product is the relationship it keeps working over the days and weeks after — not one conversation.

Messaging
A 7-day window
Open for as long as an appointment is upcoming, plus seven days after it's marked seen — long enough to still flag a delayed test result, short enough that messaging never quietly becomes an unbounded inbox. Either side can flag a conversation for admin review at any point, without blocking or notifying the other person.
Diagnosis
A second, deliberate field
The doctor's confirmed diagnosis is stored completely separately from the AI's own primary_diagnosis — never auto-filled from it. A patient only ever sees a named diagnosis once an actual licensed doctor has typed one in and saved it.
Results
Visible even after "seen"
A doctor's queue filters out appointments already marked seen, by default. But test results routinely land days later. A dedicated panel and stat chip surface exactly those results, with a push notification, so nothing uploaded late gets silently missed.
Pre-visit
A second intake, per appointment
Not the same wizard that starts a new AI session — a short check-in tied to an already-booked visit: current symptoms, medication changes, and the same AI-recommended test panel, uploadable right there.

10 — oversightInside the admin console

The admin console isn't a patient or doctor feature at all — it's for whoever is responsible for the AI's behavior and the data it holds. Nine pages, all gated behind role="admin", both server-side on every API call and client-side on the page shell itself.

PageWhat it's for
OverviewCost and usage at a glance, system health, model config, voice/translation usage — with a USD/INR display toggle.
EvidenceAccuracy, cost-effectiveness, and safety-recall compared against a traditional single-shot baseline.

AuditA filterable, append-only log of every sensitive action — no update or delete path exists for it anywhere in the codebase.
DSRA GDPR-style data-subject erasure/export request queue, with a feature-flagged real-erase flow behind it.
ReportsThe review queue for message threads either a patient or doctor has flagged — open, reviewed, or dismissed.
UsersSignup timeseries, engagement, and cohort retention analytics.
Architecture ValidationMaps the actor-critic-router design against a peer-reviewed multi-agent diagnosis benchmark.
SOPAn operator runbook — trigger, meaning, action, and escalate-if, for real situations an admin actually hits.

Live, not offline

Cost and latency figures come from a durable Postgres table logging every LLM call — model configured vs. model actually served (after any rate-limit fallback), token counts, latency, and which of four call sites made it: the doctor turn, the compressor, the critic, or the closing turn. A second table tracks voice/translation calls the same way. Both are tagged by source, so an offline evaluation run never pollutes a production cost figure.

11 — persistenceData model

A relational schema under Postgres (SQLite locally, same schema either way), grouped by what each table is actually for.

GroupTables
IdentityA shared login row per account, with 1:1 patient/doctor profile extensions, dependents, and refresh-token rotation.
Clinical sessionThe durable record of a completed AI interview, prescriptions, referrals, second opinions, refill requests, and per-appointment intake.
SchedulingAppointments — with a partial unique index making double-booking structurally impossible — slots, blocked dates, reschedule proposals, waitlists.
MessagingThreads and messages between a patient and doctor, plus reported-conversation rows awaiting admin review.
PlatformThe append-only audit log, erasure requests, uploaded files, and two usage-event tables — one for LLM calls, one for voice/translation calls.

12 — access controlAuth, end to end

Short-lived signed JWT access tokens (15 minutes) plus longer-lived, rotatable refresh tokens (7 days) — three roles: patient, doctor, admin. A 401 on an expired access token triggers a silent refresh-and-retry in the frontend before ever showing a "session expired" prompt, so an active user never has to notice their token aged out mid-task.

13 — the language layerTen languages, one model

A patient describing chest pain in Kannada or a parent reporting a child's fever in Bengali shouldn't need a translator to use this. DiffDx handles that through Sarvam AI — an Indian-language model provider — covering ten languages without changing a single line of the diagnostic logic itself.

The rule is simple: the doctor LLM always sees English. Translation happens at the API boundary, not inside the prompt. A patient's answer in Tamil is translated to English before the session ever passes it to the model; the model's question is translated back and synthesised as audio in the session's language. Prompts, exemplars, and every scoring pass stay English-only — a future swap of the translation provider touches two functions, not the whole pipeline.

Why translate at the boundary, not in the prompt

The reasoning model never has to be multilingual. Every exemplar, every prompt, every critic score stays in English regardless of what language the patient is actually speaking — so quality doesn't degrade language by language, and the ten-language list can grow without retouching the diagnostic logic at all.

16 — in hindsightDecisions that paid off

ChoiceWhy it mattered
Safety over probability in routingAny emergency-level pattern routes first, regardless of overall differential confidence. False negatives on emergencies cost lives; false positives cost a same-day visit.
Never derive the confirmed diagnosis from the AIKeeping the doctor's field separate — never pre-filled from primary_diagnosis — means a patient's named diagnosis always required an actual human decision, not a rubber stamp.
A grace window, not a fixed clockMessaging closes on appointment status plus a 7-day grace period, not a blanket "6 hours before, 12 after" rule — chosen specifically because a fixed window would have cut off patients flagging delayed test results.
The critic runs live, not offline-onlyThe same scoring machinery evaluates real sessions in production and simulated ones during regression testing — one engine, both jobs, so quality isn't only checked at eval time.
Client-side admin page guardA server-side route guard can't work for a plain browser navigation (no auth header on it) — so the admin page shells redirect via a cached-role check before any content loads, on top of every API call already being gated.

17 — honestlyWhat's still open

  • Session state is Redis-backed but falls back silently to memory if unset — a single-instance deployment without Redis loses an in-progress conversation on restart.
  • Free-tier deployment trade-offs are real — cold starts on the web service, a time-limited database plan. Fine for a demo, not for anything that needs to always respond instantly.
  • Evidence and safety-recall figures are directional — a small evaluation set is enough to catch regressions, not to make a statistical claim.
  • No EHR/FHIR integration, no real identity verification, no real payment or insurance flow — the booking and records system is real, the surrounding healthcare-business integrations are not yet.

Status: the three-loop pipeline complete, the product surface (booking, messaging, admin console) in active development, EHR/payment integration not started.

The idea that actually holds this together isn't any single feature — it's that nothing downstream of the AI is allowed to quietly inherit its authority. The critic checks the actor. The report doesn't say what the differential says. The doctor's diagnosis is a separate field the doctor has to actually fill in. The admin console watches the system, not the patients. Every one of those is the same rule, applied at a different layer: propose, don't decide.
